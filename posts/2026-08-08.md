# August 8, 2026

Yesterday I ended by saying that I know what I should do, that I could write the correct steps out for someone else in five minutes in a nice font and that I sit with the same five minutes and do none of it. I thought that was a private defect. It turns out it is a two thousand year old argument with a Greek name and the same Aristotle who told me a door is either open or closed spent a good part of the seventh book of his Ethics on people exactly like me. So I went back for the next page.

Socrates believed people like me do not exist. In the Protagoras he argues that nobody who knows or even believes that a better course is open to them will keep walking the worse one, that it is simply not in human nature to move toward what you understand to be bad (Plato, 1956). If you stayed in bed, if you never opened the notebook, then somewhere you did the math wrong, you mismeasured, you did not really know. He said it would be strange for knowledge to sit inside a person and get dragged around like a slave by everything else. And I understand why this idea has survived so long because it is a comforting one. Under the Socratic rule nobody ever watches herself do the wrong thing in full daylight. There is only ignorance and ignorance can be fixed with information, a book, a plan, a nicer font.

Aristotle read that and wrote what is now one of my favorite sentences in philosophy, that this view plainly contradicts the observed facts (Aristotle, 1925). Look around in other words. People do it constantly. He gave the condition its name akrasia, weakness of will and then made the kind of distinction he always makes. There are two ways of having knowledge, having it and using it. A man asleep or drunk can have knowledge in him that is not awake and the akratic person has knowledge the way a drunk recites the verses of Empedocles, every word in the right order, the meaning switched off (Aristotle, 1925). That is the exact description and I have no improvement to offer. I know my correct steps the way a drunk knows poetry. I can recite them beautifully. They are not on.

In 1972 two psychologists, Howard Rachlin and Leonard Green, gave pigeons a choice between two seconds of food immediately and four seconds of food after a short wait and the pigeons took the small immediate one every single time, invariably is the word the paper uses. Then they pushed the whole choice further away in time so the pigeon was deciding in advance for its future self and the preference flipped, from a distance the birds chose the bigger later reward. Given a key they could peck early to remove the tempting option from their future entirely, they pecked it (Rachlin and Green, 1972). So wanting turns out to be a matter of distance. Up close the small thing looks enormous the way near objects do and from far away the sizes are true again. The pigeon at a distance and the pigeon up close want different things and they are the same pigeon. I fall asleep as the far pigeon and wake up as the near one, which is what I was trying to say yesterday about the hopeful girl and the body at six.

And in case a pigeon feels far from your life, the human numbers say the same thing. Sheeran and Webb went through decades of studies on the gap between intending and doing and found that roughly half of the people who sincerely intend to do a thing never do it and that the gap is not made by people who never meant it, it is made almost entirely by the ones who meant it completely. They even have a name, inclined abstainers (Sheeran and Webb, 2016). Piers Steel's big review of procrastination found it has little to do with laziness and a lot to do with impulsiveness, with how loud the near thing is (Steel, 2007). And Sirois and Pychyl showed that procrastination is not about avoiding the task at all, it is about repairing a mood. You are not dodging the work, you are dodging the feeling of the work, buying ten minutes of feeling okay and billing it to a person who does not exist yet (Sirois and Pychyl, 2013). I have read many descriptions of myself this year. That one might be the most accurate.

There is one trick the research keeps standing behind and what convinces me is how stupid it is. Peter Gollwitzer calls it an implementation intention, one sentence of the form if situation X then I do Y. Across ninety-four studies that sentence made people substantially more likely to do what they already intended (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006). In one British study the motivational reading did nothing at all, thirty five percent of the motivated group exercised that week against thirty eight of the control group while the group that wrote a single sentence naming a time and a place went to ninety one percent (Milne, Orbell and Sheeran, 2002). Look at what the sentence really is. It is the pigeon's early key. You make the choice while the temptation is still far away and the sizes are still true and you tie it to a cue so that when the moment arrives there is nothing left to discuss with the near pigeon. The choice was already made by the far one, the one who could see.

So Socrates says I am ignorant, Aristotle says I am asleep in one particular spot, the pigeons say I am standing too close and the psychologists say I am paying for my mood with money borrowed from a future girl. Aristotle's verdict is the kindest and I am taking it. The honest ending is that I was supposed to write my one sentence tonight and instead I spent the whole night reading studies about why I do not do the thing, which is just a fancier way of not doing the thing. The five minute list is still sitting where it was yesterday. It is almost six in the morning as I finish this, the exact hour I keep writing about, so we are about to find out which pigeon I am. I already know.
