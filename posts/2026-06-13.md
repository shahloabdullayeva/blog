# June 13, 2026

Simone Weil said attention, taken to its highest degree, is the same thing as prayer. That it is the rarest and purest form of generosity, and that almost no one is capable of it. I think about this on the busy nights, the ones where the messages never stop, because those are exactly the nights I give my whole attention away to strangers because of work and keep none of it for myself. That is the attention work is owed. The other kind, the rare kind Weil meant, you don't owe to anyone. You only ever hand it to what you love.

Olivia's album "you seem pretty sad for a girl so in love" dropped today and I have been living inside of it. My top songs are Drop Dead, Honeybee, Expectations and The Cure and the two she let out ahead were already masterpieces. 

And it got me thinking about the rest of my hours. I know I should be using my free time learning something, since 17 to 25 is the "learning age." I know I should have started Russian before I apply to PM jobs. I know I should be practising my Persian, going to play volleyball, finishing the projects I started and left unfinished, doing my literary readings, cooking for myself instead of buying food every day, even giving more of my free time to earning more. But this job already takes 60 plus hours a week and the truth is I never once think I should be reading more books. Every other thing on that list is a should. Reading is the one thing nobody has to push me toward.

A few months ago I thought differently and now there is no pressure from anyone including me. I may be working too much and drowning in uni and some days I do feel like I am wasting my life but every time I open a good book, anything from Poe I remember why I love it this much. There is no greater pleasure  than reading a good novel. Everything else I do, I do because I should. Reading I do because I cannot help it.

Which is why Victoria by Knut Hamsun has been sitting on my desk for 2 weeks. 

It is a love story and like all the real ones it is a story about duty winning. Johannes is the millers son, Victoria is the daughter of the manor and they love each other from childhood. He grows into a poet and she is the whole reason he writes. But she is meant for Otto a wealthy lieutenant because her family needs the match and so love and duty stand on opposite sides of her for the entire book. They confess it once and she even breaks the engagement. Her father puts it back. She marries the duty and dies of the other thing, tuberculosis, leaving Johannes a final letter she could only send once it was too late to cost her anything.

That is the part that wrecks me. Love in that book is never allowed to be a should. It is the thing all the shoulds are built to keep out, and it survives anyway, underground, until it kills her. Hamsun knew exactly what it was:

Yes, what was love? A wind sighing through the roses, no, a yellow phosphorescence in the blood. Love was a music hot as hell, the kind that sets even old men's hearts to dancing. It was like the daisy that opens wide at the coming of night, and like the anemone that closes at a breath and dies at a touch. Such was love.

It could ruin its man, raise him up again, and brand him anew; it could love me today, you tomorrow, and him tomorrow night, so inconstant was it. But it could also hold fast like an unbreakable seal and blaze on, unquenchable, to the very hour of death, so eternal was it. What, then, was love?

Love is a summer night with stars in the sky and fragrance on the earth. But why does it send the young man down hidden roads, and why does it set the old man on tiptoe in his lonely room? Ah, love turns the human heart into a garden of fungus, a rank and shameless garden where secretive, insolent toadstools stand. Does it not send the monk slinking into locked gardens to lay his eye to the windows of the sleeping at night? Does it not fill the nun with folly and darken the princess's reason? It strikes the king's head down to the road until his hair sweeps all the dust of the way and beneath it he whispers shameless words to himself and laughs and sticks out his tongue. Thus was love.

No, no, it was something else again, and it was like no other thing in all the world. It came one spring night to the earth, when a young man saw two eyes, two eyes. He stared and saw. He kissed a mouth, and it was as if two lights met in his heart, a sun flashing toward a star. He fell into an embrace, and then he heard and saw nothing more in all the world.

Love is God's first word, the first thought that sailed through his mind. When he said, Let there be light, there was love. And all that he had made was very good, and he would have none of it undone. And love became the origin of the world and the ruler of the world; but all its roads are full of flowers and blood, flowers and blood.

*Knut Hamsun, Victoria*
