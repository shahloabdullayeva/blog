# July 19, 2026

I learnt a new writing technique. When you don't know where to start because there is too much to say and all of it has to be filtered before it goes out in public, it works like this. You take an abstract feeling, you find a mundane object that already carries that feeling culturally and you put the two together. I learnt it from Taylor and I believe many writers use it as well.

In Fortnight, which is one of my favourite songs of hers, there are two lines:

> All my mornings are Mondays stuck in an endless February
> I took the miracle move-on drug, the effects were temporary

and not one word in them is the word she means. Monday is dread. February is endlessness. The miracle move-on drug is whatever was supposed to be healing her and then the last word of the line takes it back. What she is saying is that she was depressed. What she wrote is a weekday, a month and a medicine that does not exist.

I assumed this was just instinct, the way some people are simply good at it and then I found out it has had a name for a hundred years. T. S. Eliot, the poet who wrote The Waste Land, called it the objective correlative: find the thing that produces the feeling and you never have to state the feeling, you let the reader do the math.

But the rule as I first understood it was missing the half that does the damage and it took me a while to understand it. Monday already carries dread, February already carries grey. Choosing them is only selection and anyone can select. What makes the line land is that she then breaks the object's scale. Monday stops being one day in seven and becomes all of them and February, the shortest month there is, is given no end at all. The borrowed object supplies the feeling. Bending it out of its proper size supplies the pressure. (Sorry, can't remember where I got that, will add the source next time.)

I have been turning this over for a practical reason, which is that there are things I would like to write down and cannot write down and I have been looking for the door. Here is the situation in general terms, since general terms are all I am offering. There is a category of afternoon in which everyone assembled agrees, warmly and repeatedly, that the matter under discussion is yours alone to decide and then decides it. Nobody in the room is lying. That is what makes it so difficult to describe afterwards. Your consent is treated as indispensable and never as decisive; it is solicited the way a formality is solicited, which is to say that the answer is anticipated rather than awaited and a refusal, when it is offered clearly and more than once, is received as evidence of good breeding rather than of intention. What I keep arriving at is that this is not cruelty. It would be so much easier if it were cruelty. It is love working from a map drawn before I existed, by people who cannot see that the territory has changed and who will grieve me either way, either because I go or because I stay and turn into someone they do not recognise. There is no version where nobody loses anything. I have checked. I have checked more than once with the fan off, because the noise makes it worse.

So I learnt the technique and then declined to use it, which I realise is a strange thing to do after four paragraphs of admiring it. I had a good object. It worked. And then I read it back and could feel myself making the thing elegant so that it would be bearable to look at and I did not want that trade tonight. But there is more than one way to keep a piece of writing from being read by everybody who can see it. An object is one. A register is another.
